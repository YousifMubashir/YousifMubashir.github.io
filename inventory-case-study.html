Data Analysis Case Study

Inventory Optimization & Demand Planning

Developing a data-driven approach to inventory planning, reorder quantities, product prioritization, and truck capacity utilization for a packing and packaging company.

Excel Data Cleaning Inventory Analysis ABC Analysis Demand Analysis

Overview

A packing and packaging company was facing difficulties determining appropriate reorder quantities for its products. Some products were being overstocked while others could run out relatively quickly.

The objective was to analyze historical sales and inventory data and develop a more structured approach to inventory planning and reorder decisions.

Scope: Approximately five months of historical data, analyzed using Microsoft Excel.

The Business Problem

The company did not have a reliable method for determining how much of each product should be ordered.

The main challenge was finding a balance between avoiding unnecessary inventory accumulation and maintaining enough stock to satisfy demand.

Data Challenges

The analysis began with a difficult data extraction and preparation process. The ERP system did not provide the required information in a clean analytical structure.

  • Sales data was separated into multiple monthly files.
  • Sales records did not contain a usable date field.
  • Sales data did not contain the required product classification.
  • The ERP filtering functionality was not sufficient for the required analysis.
  • Some products appeared multiple times.
  • Some similar product names represented different products.
  • Some products were no longer active.
  • Negative sales values required investigation.

The raw dataset contained approximately 1,300 records. After filtering and cleaning, the analysis focused on more than 330 relevant records.

Data Cleaning & Validation

Because the sales data did not contain the required product classification, the inventory dataset was used to identify General Prints and separate them from Special Prints.

Duplicate product records were consolidated where appropriate, while discontinued and irrelevant products were removed from the analysis.

Negative sales values were not simply deleted. They were investigated with a company representative and confirmed to represent returned products.

This process highlighted an important principle of the project: data cleaning required business context, not only technical manipulation of spreadsheets.

Demand & Reorder Model

After cleaning the data, historical sales were analyzed to estimate product demand and support reorder decisions.

The available dataset was used to calculate recent sales averages. For future system implementation, I recommended using Average Daily Demand so the model could adapt more effectively to recent sales activity.

Required Stock = Average Daily Demand × Coverage Days

Reorder Quantity = Required Stock − Current Inventory

Coverage periods were adjusted according to product importance rather than applying one identical target to every product.

  • Category A: approximately 60 days
  • Category B: approximately 30–45 days
  • Category C: approximately 30 days

ABC Analysis

Products were classified using ABC Analysis based on their contribution to total sales.

Products were ranked according to their percentage of total sales and then classified according to their cumulative contribution.

This allowed the company to prioritize inventory management according to actual business importance instead of treating every product equally.

Truck Capacity Optimization

Inventory planning also had to account for a practical logistical constraint: the standard truck could carry approximately 1,500 cartons.

Simply filling unused capacity with arbitrary products could result in unnecessary inventory. To address this, I calculated a statistical weight for each product based on its share of total sales during the most recent 60-day period.

Product Weight = Product Sales ÷ Total Sales

Products with stronger sales performance therefore received a greater share of any remaining truck capacity.

Unexpected Order Scenario

The normal ordering process was designed around the standard truck capacity, but unexpected customer requirements could require a more flexible approach.

I proposed an "Unexpected Order" option supported by a smaller 500-carton truck. This would allow the company to respond to urgent customer requirements without unnecessarily filling a larger truck.

Where additional capacity remained necessary, the remaining space could be allocated using the statistical product weights.

Anomaly Detection & Business Validation

During the review of the cleaned data, I identified a product whose sales and inventory behavior appeared inconsistent with the other General Print products.

Rather than assuming the data was correct, I investigated the discrepancy with a company representative.

The company confirmed that the product had been incorrectly classified as a General Print in the ERP system. It was actually a Special Print.

Potential financial impact: the incorrect classification could have resulted in an unnecessary purchase of approximately 30 million IQD under the existing automated purchasing logic.

This finding demonstrated the importance of validating analytical results against real business context.

The model would therefore recommend ordering 95 cartons to restore the product to the desired coverage level.

This is a hypothetical example created to demonstrate the methodology and does not represent actual company data.

Business Recommendations

  • Improve the structure and reliability of ERP data exports.
  • Use demand-based reorder calculations.
  • Apply different coverage targets based on product importance.
  • Use ABC classification to prioritize inventory management.
  • Use sales-based weights when allocating remaining truck capacity.
  • Introduce an alternative process for unexpected customer orders.
  • Validate unusual analytical results against business context.

Implementation

The analysis and proposed inventory logic were presented to company management.

The resulting algorithm was subsequently sent to the company's ERP software provider for integration into the system.

At the time of this case study, implementation was still in progress, so no unsupported claims about final savings or operational improvements are made.
